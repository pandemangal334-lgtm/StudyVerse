1. Business Model Canvas (BMC)

Definition: A one-page strategic tool developed by Alexander Osterwalder to map how a business creates, delivers and captures value.

Remember: It has 9 interconnected building blocks.

See all 9 blocks
  1. Customer Segments: groups the business serves.
  2. Value Propositions: products/services that solve customer problems.
  3. Channels: how value is communicated, sold and delivered.
  4. Customer Relationships: how the business interacts with customers.
  5. Revenue Streams: how the business earns money.
  6. Key Resources: essential physical, intellectual, human and financial assets.
  7. Key Activities: essential actions, such as product development and marketing.
  8. Key Partnerships: suppliers, vendors and strategic partners.
  9. Cost Structure: expenses of operating the business.
Steps to build a BMC
  1. Understand the business idea and problem.
  2. Identify customer segments.
  3. Define the value proposition.
  4. Map channels and customer relationships.
  5. Identify revenue streams.
  6. List key resources and activities.
  7. Establish key partnerships.
  8. Estimate costs.
  9. Create and refine the canvas.
Benefits and examples

Benefits: holistic view, iterative improvement, early risk identification and scalability.

E-commerce example: online shoppers; convenient selection and pricing; website/social media; personalised support; product sales/subscriptions/ads; platform and inventory; suppliers and logistics partners.

SaaS example: small and medium businesses; scalable cloud software; website/app/direct sales; online support and updates; subscriptions/upgrades; development team and cloud infrastructure; integration and cloud providers.

Ola example from PDF: riders and driver-partners; on-demand mobility; app; ride commissions, surge pricing and Ola Money; technology platform and driver network; vehicle makers, insurers and payment gateways.

2. Lean Startup Methodology

Meaning: An iterative approach to launching a business that prioritises learning, efficiency, customer needs and adaptability.

Build–Measure–Learn loop
  • Build: create a Minimum Viable Product (MVP), the smallest version that tests a core idea.
  • Measure: collect real-user data such as usage, conversion, feedback and willingness to pay.
  • Learn: analyse evidence, then pivot (change direction) or persevere (continue).

The loop repeats, helping a startup learn quickly without over-investing.

Validated learning

Test assumptions through real-world experiments. Evidence may confirm or challenge beliefs about the market, customer needs and product features.

How it helps entrepreneurs
  1. Risk mitigation: test early before large investment.
  2. Efficient resource use: save time and capital with an MVP.
  3. Customer-centric approach: use customer feedback.
  4. Adaptability and innovation: improve quickly as conditions change.
  5. Data-driven decisions: rely on measured evidence rather than assumptions.
Examples in PDF

Paytm: expanded from recharge/bill payments toward broader digital payments. Meesho: moved from deals discovery to social-commerce reselling. CRED: began with an invite-only group to test its hypothesis.

3. Revenue Models

Ways a business earns income.

Types and examples
  • Commission/transaction: earns a cut per transaction — Zomato, Swiggy.
  • Subscription: recurring fee — Disney+ Hotstar, ZEE5.
  • Freemium: free core features, paid premium — Truecaller, LinkedIn.
  • Advertising: advertisers pay for attention — MX Player, Google (as stated in PDF).
  • Licensing/SaaS: software or technology access fees — Zoho.
  • Direct-to-Consumer (D2C): sells directly to end users — Mamaearth, boAt.

4. Cost Structures

Types
  • Fixed costs: stay constant regardless of output, e.g. rent, salaries, server hosting.
  • Variable costs: change with volume, e.g. delivery and payment-gateway fees.
  • Cost-driven: minimise costs to compete on price. PDF example: IndiGo.
  • Value-driven: prioritise premium value and brand experience over lowest cost.
100 Rupees Challenge

Students/teams use ₹100 and a short time window (often 24–48 hours) to launch a micro-venture and maximise returns. Apply bootstrapping and Build–Measure–Learn: test a small offer, measure customer response and adjust quickly.

5. Company Structures in India

Legal structure affects liability, taxation, compliance and fundraising.

StructureLiabilityTypical fit (PDF)
Sole ProprietorshipUnlimitedSmall local businesses
Partnership FirmUnlimited, sharedSmall professional teams
LLPLimitedProfessional services
OPCLimitedSolo founder wanting liability protection
Private LimitedLimitedHigh-growth / VC-funded startup
Public LimitedLimitedLarge-scale fundraising / public markets
Private Limited Company
  • Separate legal entity; can sue and be sued in its own name.
  • Shares have transfer restrictions; membership is limited to 200 (except OPC); cannot invite the public to subscribe to securities (as summarised in PDF).
  • Continuous existence despite death/resignation of a member.
  • Company owns its property; a person may be shareholder, employee and director.
  • PDF notes strong borrowing and equity-funding potential, with higher compliance.
LLP
  • Separate legal entity; partners generally have limited liability.
  • PDF highlights flexible operations, no minimum capital contribution, two or more partners, and comparatively lower compliance/registration cost.
OPC
  • One member; corporate status with limited liability.
  • Designed for a solo entrepreneur to own and manage a company.
Sole Proprietorship
  • Wholly controlled by one person; owner and business are not separate legal entities in the PDF description.
  • Simple and flexible, but owner bears business liabilities.
Partnership Firm
  • Two or more people share profits; a partnership deed sets investment, profit-sharing and operating terms.
  • Partners share responsibility and have unlimited liability (as stated in PDF).
Public Limited Company
  • PDF describes a public company as one that is not a private company.
  • Can raise substantial capital through public markets; shareholder liability is limited to their stake.
Other structures and decision tree

The PDF also lists Section 8 Company (NGOs/non-profits) and Producer Company (farmer cooperatives).

  1. Solo + wants limited liability → OPC; solo + liability protection not priority → Sole Proprietorship.
  2. Co-founders + VC/angel funding planned → Private Limited.
  3. No external equity + professional services → LLP; otherwise → Partnership Firm.
  4. IPO/large public fundraising planned → Public Limited at that stage.

Note: the source PDF includes legal/company-registration summaries and some potentially time-sensitive details. Use it for this chapter's revision; verify current law before real-world registration decisions.

6. Patents, Trademarks & Copyright

IP typeProtects
PatentNew invention or technical process; PDF states usually 20 years.
TrademarkDistinctive brand name, logo or symbol identifying goods/services.
CopyrightOriginal creative expression: writing, music, software code, films; not the idea itself.
Three case studies from the PDF

Patent — Novartis AG v Union of India (2013): the Supreme Court denied a patent for an updated version of Glivec under Section 3(d), addressing evergreening and genuine therapeutic improvement.

Trademark — Whirlpool Co. v N.R. Dongre (1996): the case is described as establishing trans-border reputation, allowing protection based on global brand reputation.

Copyright — Eastern Book Company v D.B. Modak (2008): the Court required a “modicum of creativity,” not merely labour, for copyright originality.

7. Exit Strategies

Definition: How founders and early investors convert their ownership stake into cash or liquid assets.

Types and examples
  • IPO: company lists shares on a public stock exchange. PDF examples: Zomato and Nykaa listings in 2021.
  • Acquisition / M&A: a larger company buys the startup. PDF example: Walmart's 2018 acquisition of a majority stake in Flipkart.
  • Secondary sale: existing shareholders sell shares to new investors; company itself does not raise fresh capital from that sale.
  • Buyback: company repurchases shares from investors or employees.
  • Liquidation / wind-down: assets are sold and operations close.
